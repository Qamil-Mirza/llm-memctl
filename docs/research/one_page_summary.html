Learned memory control for long conversations: what we set out to do, and what we found

One-page summary · 7 October 2026 · Details and every number: EXPERIMENTS.md §1–§19d and docs/research/THESIS_RESULTS_OUTLINE.md

Aim

A language model answering questions about a long history (weeks of chat) cannot read all of it at once. A memory controller decides what stays in view, what goes to an archive, and what is searched back when a question arrives. We asked whether a learned controller (imitation of a hindsight teacher, then reinforcement learning) does better than simple rules at the same token budget. We tested on synthetic tasks, then on two real benchmarks (LoCoMo, LongMemEval), with a frozen 7B reader (Qwen2.5-7B) and an official judge. Every paid test was pre-registered: its success rule was written down and passed a free check before it ran.

Research questions

  • RQ1. Does a learned controller beat simple rules under the same budget?
  • RQ2. Can a controller learn from hindsight (what later turned out to be needed)?
  • RQ3. Does reinforcement learning (GRPO) add anything to imitation?
  • RQ4. Does it hold up with real models and conversations, and when is it worth it?

What we did, and why it worked or not

Answers

  • RQ1: Yes, but only at the question. On synthetic tasks a learned controller wins clearly. On real conversations, deciding what to keep beforehand does not beat rules; choosing which retrieved turns the reader sees does: +0.047 over the best rule, then +0.045 more by showing eight turns.
  • RQ2: On synthetic tasks yes; on real chats, not enough. Hindsight says what was needed, but on real text that cannot be predicted before the question is asked. The useful signal arrives with the question.
  • RQ3: At most about one point. The limit is how much each reader-graded sample tells the learner, not how credit is split over time.
  • RQ4: Yes, with a real 7B reader and two judges; it is worth it when the prompt stays small. With the same selector, 16 turns put more evidence in view than 8 but scored −0.057 (CI −0.096, −0.019): with the evidence present, accuracy fell from 0.68 to 0.58 at 2.6k tokens. A pre-registered repeat (second head, second host) gave −0.026 (CI −0.066, +0.015), not significant, but the same fall with the evidence present (0.71 to 0.62). LongMemEval is a selection problem; LoCoMo is a search problem (evidence spread over many short turns).

Limitations

  • One reader and one frozen prompt; the ~3k-token onset of context rot is measured for that pair only.
  • The positive reader result is pre-registered on LongMemEval only. The LoCoMo pick (5 turns) missed (+0.023, CI −0.001, +0.048). The LoCoMo win of 16 turns over FIFO (0.344 vs 0.295 at a quarter of the tokens) is exploratory.
  • The context-rot accuracy loss was significant in one of two runs; its confirmation missed. Both judges are 8B-class.
  • LoCoMo intervals rest on only 10 conversations. The §15 arm was the best of three before it was replicated.

Future work

  • A larger, pre-registered test of the context-rot pair (more questions or seeds), since one confirmation missed.
  • A second reader of another family or size, to see whether the onset moves.
  • A chooser that reaches the per-question ceiling (half the tokens at equal evidence).
  • LoCoMo as a search problem: better retrieval units (fact keys, session-level search) and heads trained on LoCoMo.
  • A leave-one-out set reward for GRPO (declared, not run).
Total GPU spend for all experiments: about $8.63 (RunPod A40, every launch approved in advance). Accuracy is the share of questions the judge marks correct; intervals are paired 95% bootstrap intervals.
