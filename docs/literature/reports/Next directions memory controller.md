# Fix the second hop before tuning GRPO

## In plain words

**What we found.** Our small "memory manager" decides what a 7-billion-parameter language model keeps in front of it, what it files away in an archive, and what it pulls back out. With only 2% of the conversation allowed in view, it gets 83% of questions right. A cheating version that knows the future gets 94%. Almost every point of that gap comes from one mistake: the needed fact was filed in the archive, and the manager never pulled it back out. When we looked at the logs, most of those misses were the same kind of question. These are "two-step" questions, like "what is the priority of the courier of sensor-171?" To answer it you first need to know who the courier is (clerk-172), and then look up clerk-172's priority. Our archive search only uses the words in the question. The question never says "clerk-172", so the search can't find the second fact. More training of the same kind (GRPO, a trial-and-error method) couldn't fix this, because the right fact usually wasn't on the list of things the manager was allowed to pull back.

**Why it matters.** It means the next gain is mostly not about smarter training. It is about giving the manager a way to search for the fact it actually needs. If we fix the search, the trial-and-error training finally has something to learn about. Until then, extra training mostly reshuffles the same choices.

**What we do next.** First, a few cheap checks that need no rented GPU. Then a "follow the clue" search that uses the first fact to find the second. Then small changes to how GRPO scores its trial runs, so it learns from real differences and not from noise. Last, a way to give credit to the single decision that won or lost each question, instead of spreading one score across 200 decisions.

---

**The learned controller's remaining 0.105 gap to the oracle at a 2% budget is a retrieval-reach problem, not mainly an optimisation problem, so the best next steps are retrieval fixes that widen the action space, followed by GRPO changes that only pay off once that space contains the right item.** The Experiment 9 failure logs show that about **75% of regret-BC's `archived_not_retrieved` failures are two-hop questions**, and about **190 per seed (≈0.097 of all 1,952 queries) are misses of the second hop while the first-hop fact sits in active memory**. The oracle misses that case only 9 times. The controller can only retrieve from a shortlist of 8 lexical BM25 matches to the question text, and the regret expert labels retrievals only inside that shortlist. So no amount of imitation or GRPO can reach a bridge-entity fact that the question's words don't match. This explains the flat GRPO result (−0.004) better than the GRPO-specific hypotheses in the notes. Ranked by value per effort, the recommendations are: (1) three log-and-replay diagnostics; (2) bridge-entity (iterative) retrieval plus a hindsight-trained reranker; (3) GRPO fixes, namely batch-level advantage scaling, a kept-on expert loss, and per-budget stratification; (4) per-question credit assignment, which the deterministic simulator gives almost for free. Several literature claims behind these choices were not re-verified by the research team and are flagged inline.

## The gap is two-hop questions the search cannot reach

Experiment 9 sets the target. With Qwen2.5-7B reading, full context scores 0.898. Regret-BC scores 0.832 at a 2% budget (−0.066 [−0.081, −0.050] vs full context). Oracle-approx scores 0.937, and the oracle-minus-regret gap is +0.105 [+0.088, +0.122]. GRPO fine-tuning from regret-BC changed success by −0.004 [−0.016, +0.008] ([EXPERIMENTS.md §9](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). Per seed, regret-BC logs **286, 306 and 319** `archived_not_retrieved` failures, against **72 for the oracle**, out of 1,952 queries. The excess (~230 queries, ≈0.118) accounts for the whole oracle gap (`runs/exp9b_qwen7b_controllers/*__fraction0.02/summary.json`).

Breaking the failure records down by question category and by which evidence piece was missing changes the diagnosis. This is a new analysis for this report, made from `failures.jsonl`:

| controller (2% budget) | ANR, two-hop | ANR, one-hop | ANR where only hop 2 missing | only hop 1 missing | both missing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| regret-BC s0 / s1 / s2 | 219 / 228 / 222 | 67 / 78 / 97 | **186 / 194 / 188** | 75 / 84 / 99 | 25 / 28 / 32 |
| regret-BC → GRPO s0 | 225 | 78 | **209** | 84 | 10 |
| oracle-approx | 51 | 21 | **9** | 50 | 13 |
| salience-archive heuristic | 214 | 303 | 166 | 323 | 28 |

The dominant failure is concrete. A sample record has the question "what is the priority of the courier of sensor-171?" Hop 1 ("the courier of sensor-171 is clerk-172") is active. Hop 2 ("the priority of clerk-172 is M37M") is archived. The two share only the word "priority" with the question. The code explains why the policy cannot recover. `RLController` builds its retrieval shortlist by searching the archive for the *question text* (`retrieve_candidates: 8`), then makes a Bernoulli keep/skip decision per shortlist item (`memctl/controllers/rl.py`). The regret expert's retrieval label is defined only over that shortlist (`memctl/rl/expert.py`). If the hop-2 item ranks below 8th among the hundreds of archived items that mention "priority", it is outside the action space for the policy, for DAgger and for GRPO. The oracle bypasses this because it retrieves by hindsight. GRPO's one visible behavioural change fits the picture. It retrieved more (17.5 vs 14.3 items per episode) at lower precision (0.87 vs 0.93), so the "both missing" cases fell. But hop-2-only misses *rose*, from 186 to 209.

What is not yet established: the per-step archive state and shortlist are logged only for 2 detail episodes per cell, so the share of hop-2 items that fall outside the top-8 could not be measured at scale. The three logged cases split one in the top 3, one at rank 4–8 and one beyond 8. That is anecdotal. Measuring it properly is diagnostic 1 below.

## Three cheap diagnostics settle what GRPO can and cannot fix

**Diagnostic 1: shortlist recall by hop.** Replay the 2% episodes for regret-BC with the scripted reader. The environment is deterministic per seed, and no LLM calls are needed to get controller behaviour. Log, at every question step, the BM25 rank of each archived evidence item. If most hop-2 items rank beyond 8, the ceiling is in the retriever, and the GRPO recommendations below are second-order until retrieval changes. If most rank within 8, the policy is choosing not to retrieve them, and credit assignment (the last section) becomes the priority. This takes a few CPU hours and a logging hook. It decides the ordering of everything else.

**Diagnostic 2: audit the GRPO signal.** The notes flagged three GRPO pathologies. The run logs already rule one out and weaken another. Entropy collapse is not the story: policy entropy in the GRPO phase stayed between **0.34 and 1.28 nats** across 60 iterations (`runs/recall_regret_bc_grpo_s0/train_log.jsonl`). The clip-higher and Clip-Cov fixes for entropy collapse ([arXiv:2505.22617](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617)) are therefore low priority. The notes' "cost-amplification" worry, where std-normalisation inflates tiny continuous cost differences, is only partly relevant. The default reward is an integer count of correct answers with −0.1 per forced fallback and no continuous cost term (`memctl/rewards.py`). The real version of the risk: when all 8 rollouts answer the same questions and one incurs a forced fallback, the 0.1 gap becomes a full-size ±1 advantage, because the code divides by group std with only a 1e‑6 floor (`memctl/rl/train.py`). Ties are also frequent by construction. Each iteration has just **3 groups** (24 episodes / G=8), and the budget fraction is drawn per group from {2, 5, 10, 25%}, where the 25% groups almost always tie. The count of decisions with non-zero advantage swings from about 1,100 to 3,300 per iteration, which suggests that two of three groups were sometimes fully tied. Meanwhile validation success at 2% stayed flat in a **0.845–0.864** band throughout. The audit should log, for each group, the spread, the budget, whether the spread came only from fallback penalties, and the advantage magnitude. Cost: an afternoon, no GPU.

**Diagnostic 3: the best-of-8 behaviour-cloning ablation.** RAFT-style filtered imitation (keep the best rollout per seed, clone it) is a strong minimal baseline. "A Minimalist Approach to LLM Reasoning" reports it competitive with GRPO, and attributes much of GRPO's edge to discarding all-wrong prompts ([arXiv:2504.11343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11343); *unverified, recalled from memory*). If best-of-8 BC matches GRPO here, the RL objective adds nothing over filtered imitation. Cost: one CPU training run.

One caveat applies to all GRPO results to date. GRPO was trained against the **scripted reader** (`agent: scripted_reader, noise: 0.0`) and evaluated with Qwen-7B (`configs/rl/grpo/recall_regret_bc_grpo_s0.yaml`). That mismatch cannot explain the retrieval gap, which shows up under both readers. But it means GRPO never saw `retrieved_but_ignored` failures, so it cannot be expected to fix them.

## Retrieval fixes come first because they enlarge the action space

**Bridge-entity (iterative) retrieval is the single highest-value change.** At each question step, run a second search whose query adds the values found in active or just-retrieved items that match the question's entity. In the example, the first pass finds the courier of sensor-171, and the second pass searches for "priority clerk-172". Then pass the union shortlist to the policy, and let the expert label it. The literature supports multi-step retrieval driven by intermediate evidence. A retriever framed as an MDP that picks the next item given what it already has, trained from LLM feedback with only ~4M extra parameters, transferred across reader LLMs and won an EMNLP 2024 outstanding paper ([arXiv:2406.14739](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14739)). StepSearch's step-wise information-gain rewards beat sparse outcome rewards by +11.2 and +4.2 points at 3B and 7B on multi-hop QA ([arXiv:2505.15107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15107)). LongMemEval's fact-augmented key expansion raised recall@k by 9.4% and QA accuracy by 5.4% ([arXiv:2410.10813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10813)). A rule-based first version needs no training and no LLM. Indexing each archived fact under its parsed (entity, attribute) key is the structured analogue of key expansion. The upper bound on payoff is the ~190 hop-2 misses per seed, about **+0.09 success at 2%**. That holds only if the reader then answers correctly. You'd know it worked when the hop-2-only count falls toward the oracle's 9 and the paired 2% interval against regret-BC excludes zero. The cost is a day of coding, plus one Qwen-7B evaluation sweep. Exp 9's full 53-cell sweep cost about $1.35 on an A40 ([EXPERIMENTS.md §9](../EXPERIMENTS.md)), so a single new controller costs cents.

**A small reranker trained from hindsight labels attacks the one-hop and hop-1 misses.** About 75–99 failures per seed miss hop 1 or a one-hop fact, against 50 for the oracle. The research notes recommend a listwise or InfoNCE loss over archive candidates, with the hindsight answer item as the positive and **older versions of the same fact as hard negatives**. Versioned "as of step N" facts are lexically near-identical, and a hashing or BM25 scorer cannot separate them. Training needs zero LLM calls and can use millions of CPU samples. A plain recency prior added to similarity achieved perfect latest-version retrieval on synthetic freshness data ([arXiv:2509.19376](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19376v1)). VersionRAG reports 90% vs 58% on version-sensitive queries ([arXiv:2510.08109](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08109v1); *verified from a search snippet only*). MemRL's two-phase "filter by similarity, then rank by learned Q-value" design keeps the LLM frozen and learns only utility ([arXiv:2601.03192](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03192)), and the 27k MLP can serve as that scorer. DeepRetrieval shows that rewarding retrieval recall directly, with no generator in the loop, works with RL too: 65.07% vs 24.68% recall on publication search ([arXiv:2503.00223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00223v3)). You'd know it worked when recall@3 of the hindsight item rises on replayed episodes before any LLM evaluation.

**An exploration slot makes never-retrieved items learnable.** Causal Memory Policy (October 2026) shows that utility cannot be identified for memories that are never retrieved. Identification fails for 54% of required memories on LongMemEval and 67% on LoCoMo. It fixes this by reserving context slots for items sampled with known propensities and then applying self-normalised IPW ([arXiv:2610.02070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02070)). Reserving one retrieval slot for a propensity-sampled archive item gives unbiased data for exactly the items that lexical search never surfaces. The same paper warns that per-query utility does not aggregate into a query-independent retention score, so the reranker's features must be query-conditioned.

**Reader-side fixes are worth an hour, not a project.** `retrieved_but_ignored` is only ~20 per seed. Placing retrieved items immediately before the question, newest version last and tagged with its step, is the cheap remedy. LongMemEval reports up to 10 points from structured JSON plus Chain-of-Note reading ([arXiv:2410.10813](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813v2)). Lost-in-the-middle effects ([arXiv:2406.16008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16008v2)) likely matter little with three items.

## GRPO changes that pay off once the right item is reachable

These changes are ordered by cost. The notes stress that nearly all the evidence comes from billion-parameter LLMs on maths and code, so transfer to a 27k-parameter, fixed-horizon controller is inference, not demonstration.

**Normalise across the batch, enlarge the batch, and stratify by budget (hours).** Dr. GRPO shows that per-group std division over-weights groups that are nearly tied ("Questions with lower standard deviations… are given higher weights"). It removes the std term to recover an unbiased Monte Carlo baseline ([arXiv:2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783)). The "Tricks or Traps?" ablation (ICLR 2026) found that a group-mean baseline with batch-level std is one of two tricks sufficient to beat GRPO and DAPO in its setting ([arXiv:2508.08221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08221); *verified from search snippets*). REINFORCE++ uses global normalisation for robustness ([arXiv:2501.03262](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03262); *snippets*). RLOO's leave-one-out mean without std is another option ([arXiv:2402.14740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740); *unverified, from memory*). UMA's Task-Stratified GRPO normalises separate groups separately ([arXiv:2602.18493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18493)). Here that means computing scale statistics within each budget fraction, so the near-always-tied 25% groups stop diluting the 2% ones. Raising episodes per iteration from 24 to at least 96 (12+ groups) is the other one-line change. It costs nothing in LLM calls, because training uses the scripted reader. You'd know it worked when the 2% validation curve leaves its 0.845–0.864 band. **The honest expectation is small gains until retrieval is fixed.**

**Keep the expert in the loss (a day).** Robotics evidence shows that naive online RL from an imitation start causes early "unlearning", and that keeping the offline data, or warming up on rollouts from the pre-trained policy, prevents it (WSRL, [arXiv:2412.07762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07762); *snippets*). LLM work converged on single-stage hybrids that keep an SFT term inside RL. CHORD up-weights SFT where the model is uncertain ([arXiv:2508.11408](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.11408); *snippets*). SRFT reports +9.0 over the best RL baseline and +3.4 over SFT→RL ([arXiv:2506.19767](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.19767); *snippets*). RSTG distils from a teacher only on all-fail groups, for +4.02% maths and +3.05% code ([arXiv:2608.00782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00782)). ReLIFT interleaves SFT on the hardest prompts ([survey, arXiv:2509.06948](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.06948); *ReLIFT's own ID 2506.07527 is unverified*). Because the regret expert is free in hindsight, the translation is GRPO loss + DAgger cross-entropy on visited states + KL to the frozen BC policy, with the expert weight raised on tied groups. The earlier literature review adds ADVISOR, which adaptively weights IL against RL by an estimated imitation gap ([arXiv:2007.12173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)). Its Poisoned Doors example warns that IL-then-RL can be strictly worse than RL alone when the teacher sees what the student cannot. That is exactly the oracle-BC failure (0.320) in Exp 9.

**Mixed-policy groups only after the action space is fixed.** LUFFY adds off-policy expert traces to GRPO groups and reports +6.4 on six maths benchmarks. It also trains weak models where on-policy RL "completely fails" ([arXiv:2504.14945](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14945); *snippets*). Putting 1–2 oracle or regret-expert rollouts into each group of 8 guarantees spread. But the oracle's decisive hop-2 retrievals are not expressible inside today's 8-item question-text shortlist. Without the retrieval fix, these members contribute advantage to actions the student cannot take. The notes label this an untested hypothesis.

**Prioritise informative seeds; don't over-sample and discard.** DAPO's dynamic sampling drops all-equal groups by over-sampling ([arXiv:2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476); *unverified, from memory*). That wastes rollouts once an LLM reader is in the loop. Prompt Replay instead re-selects prompts whose pass rate is near 0.5, which cuts zero-variance prompts and speeds early gains. The authors call it most useful when rollout compute binds ([arXiv:2603.21177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21177)). Here that means keeping a per-seed history of within-group spread and resampling frontier seeds.

**Skip the length-normalisation fixes, GSPO and GMPO.** Episodes have a fixed 200-step length, so Dr. GRPO's 1/|o| fix and DAPO's token-level loss change nothing. GSPO's sequence-level ratio ([arXiv:2507.18071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071)) would blend 200 independent decisions into one ratio and give up per-step credit. Its motivation, MoE routing noise, does not exist in a small MLP. VAPO's argument that a well-trained critic beats critic-free methods on long horizons ([arXiv:2504.05118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05118); *unverified, from memory*) is the strongest steelman for revisiting a value net, which costs nothing at 27k parameters. It conflicts with the project's earlier finding that PPO lost to GRPO from scratch, so it belongs after the credit-assignment work below.

## Per-question credit is nearly free in a replayable simulator

The current GRPO assigns one episode-level advantage to all ~200 decisions (`set_group_advantages` in `memctl/rl/train.py`). A successful retrieve therefore shares credit with ~199 unrelated decisions. The 2025–2026 agent-RL literature agrees that gains come from giving different steps different advantages through a same-state comparison. GiGPO groups actions taken from recurring "anchor states" and reports >12% over GRPO on ALFWorld and >9% on WebShop at near-zero extra cost ([arXiv:2505.10978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10978)). It "naturally degrades to GRPO" when states don't repeat ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10978)), and exact memory states diverge after the first differing decision.

**The project has a better anchor than GiGPO's: the question itself.** The synthetic stream is deterministic per seed, so all 8 rollouts in a group face the same question at the same step. The retrieval decision is made at that step, and the correctness reward lands on the same step. A per-question group advantage compares, across the 8 rollouts, whether question q was answered. Combined as in GiGPO (A = A_episode + ω·A_question), it gives the retrieve decision at q its own low-variance signal at **zero extra reader calls**. Crediting that question's outcome also to the earlier evict/archive decisions that touched its evidence items is a return-equivalent redistribution in the spirit of RUDDER ([arXiv:1806.07857](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)) and Shapley-based SCAR ([arXiv:2505.20417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20417v1)). Both keep the summed reward unchanged. This design is this report's inference. No cited paper tests it on a non-LLM memory controller. You'd know it worked when GRPO's 2% validation rises and the hop-2 failure count falls faster than under episode-level advantages, at matched episodes.

**Branching from shared prefixes is the audit tool, at moderate cost.** VinePPO resets to intermediate states and estimates values by Monte Carlo continuations. It reports that learned value nets rank alternative steps barely better than random, yet it reaches baselines' peak in up to 3.0× less wall-clock time ([arXiv:2410.01679](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). Tree-GRPO turns sibling branches into step-level groups and matched chain-based RL at a quarter of the rollout budget ([arXiv:2509.21240](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21240); *snippets*). ARPO and TreeRL branch only at high-entropy steps ([arXiv:2507.19849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19849); [arXiv:2506.11902](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11902)). Here, branch at contested archive and retrieve decisions, and truncate each branch at the next question that needs the affected item. Caching reader answers by (question, context) then brings LLM cost to 1–3 calls per branch. AggreVaTe and LOLS, cited in the earlier review, are the imitation-side analogue: roll-out-labelled per-decision regret that "can improve upon the reference policy" ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/changb15.html)). Memory-R2's local re-rollouts are their 2026 memory-specific instance ([arXiv:2605.21768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21768)).

**Shape rewards with caution.** The harness already logs hindsight terms such as `retrieval_success` (`memctl/rewards.py`), which makes a dense shaping bonus a one-line config change. PURE found that summed process rewards invite hacking, and that min-form credit largely removes it ([arXiv:2504.15275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15275)). The earlier review also found a dissent, "Coverage, Not Targeting": concentrating advantage on "progress turns or on random turns is equally harmful" ([arXiv:2609.02417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02417)). Keep the outcome term, use small weights, and prefer the redistribution above, which cannot change the optimal policy. Potential-based shaping guarantees (Ng et al., 1999) were *not re-verified*, and the hindsight "needed-again" bonus is not potential-based.

## Conclusion

GRPO's flat result looked like an optimisation puzzle. The failure logs reframe it as a reach problem. The policy, its expert and its RL fine-tuning all choose from a shortlist built from the question's words, and two-hop questions put the decisive fact where those words cannot reach. That moves every GRPO-variant recommendation from "try it now" to "try it after the action space contains the answer". It also explains why the oracle, which retrieves by hindsight rather than by search, keeps a 0.10 lead that imitation cannot close.

The most transferable idea for the thesis is that a deterministic, seed-replayable simulator turns the hardest problem in long-horizon agent RL, assigning credit across 200 decisions, into nearly free bookkeeping. Every rollout in a group faces the same question at the same step, so per-question comparisons give step-level advantages that LLM-agent papers need tree search or learned critics to get. If bridge retrieval closes most of the hop-2 gap and per-question credit then lets GRPO beat its BC start with a clean interval, the thesis gains its first evidence that RL adds something imitation cannot.
