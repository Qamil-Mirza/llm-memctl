# Retrieve every time, then learn what to keep

**The systems that do well on LoCoMo and LongMemEval all retrieve on every question. None of them learns *whether* to retrieve. Where learning appears, it decides what to write, what to evict, or which of a wide retrieved pool to show the reader.** Our controller fails at exactly the decision the field avoids. It learned a sparse retrieve gate, and on real conversations that gate almost never fires: retrieval recall is 0.00–0.03, against 0.35 (LoCoMo) and 0.65 (LongMemEval) for the "always fetch BM25 top-5" rules that beat it ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). The most valuable change is also the cheapest. Make top-k retrieval at each question unconditional, and keep the learned policy for eviction and for choosing *which* candidates fill the budget. That alone should lift the learned controller to the heuristics' level: about +0.05–0.10 judge accuracy on LoCoMo and about +0.2 on LongMemEval, by our estimate. After that, the next gains come from three places: a pick-k-of-N retrieval head trained on the gold evidence labels both benchmarks ship; a teacher that stops treating deletion as free; and better retrieval units (speaker names, dates, BM25+dense fusion, fact keys). All but the fact keys can be built and trained on a laptop. Before any of these numbers go into the thesis, the evaluation needs fixing. Our LoCoMo scores include the adversarial category the field excludes. A Qwen2.5-7B judge grades its own answers. LongMemEval has no full-context or evidence-only ceiling yet. And 100 held-out questions give confidence intervals of about ±0.10.

## In plain words

**What we found.** The best memory systems on these two tests never ask "should I look in the archive?" They always look, pull out the few best matches, and put their cleverness into choosing and arranging what the reader sees. Our small manager was trained to decide whether to look. On real conversations it almost always decided not to, so it lost to simple rules that always look.

**Why it matters.** The fix is mostly about design, not training tricks. If looking is automatic, the manager can't fail by never looking. It can then spend its effort on what it is good at: deciding what to keep in view under a tight space limit.

**What we do next.** Make looking automatic, teach the manager *which* five items to pull back using the answer locations the datasets already provide, stop the teacher from deleting things for free, and fix how we grade the answers so a thesis examiner can trust the numbers.

## Every top system retrieves on every question

Published scores fall into three tiers, and the tiers mostly reflect the reader model and who ran the test. The 2025 "memory layer" generation (Mem0, Zep, LangMem, A-Mem) used GPT-4o-mini and scored roughly **58–75 judge accuracy on LoCoMo**. Mem0's own table puts full context at 72.90, above Mem0 (66.88), Mem0g (68.44) and Zep (65.99) ([Mem0 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413)). Zep re-ran itself at 75.14, after showing that Mem0's harness had given both speakers the user role and placed timestamps inside the message text ([Zep blog](https://www.getzep.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/)). Late-2025 retrieval-heavy systems reached 82–91 on LongMemEval_S. Examples are EmergenceMem at 86.0 with GPT-4o ([Emergence blog](https://www.emergence.ai/blog/sota-on-longmemeval-with-rag)) and Hindsight at 89.0–91.4, which grades itself with a different judge from its baselines ([Hindsight paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12818v1)). The 2026 vendor claims sit at 92–96 with GPT-5-class readers ([Agent Zero Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29606); [Mem0 2026 guide](https://mem0.ai/blog/ai-memory-benchmarks-in-2026)). Almost all of those are self-reported. ByteRover's re-test puts Zep at 75.1 against Zep's claimed 94.7 ([Mem0 2026 guide](https://mem0.ai/blog/ai-memory-benchmarks-in-2026)).

Only one 2025 comparison ran many systems under one harness, and it is sobering. In LightMem's re-run, **plain NaiveRAG beat Mem0, MemoryOS and LangMem on both benchmarks with every backbone**. Full text stayed on top for LoCoMo (71.83 with GPT-4o-mini, 74.87 with Qwen3-30B) ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18866)). The two benchmarks behave differently. LoCoMo conversations are short, about 16–26k tokens, so full context is a ceiling. LongMemEval_S is about 115k tokens, and there full context is weak: 0.606 for GPT-4o and 0.454 for Llama-3.1-8B, against 0.870 and 0.710 when the reader sees only the evidence sessions ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)). Our own LoCoMo run shows the same pattern. Full context (0.510) ties the hindsight oracle (0.505) ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). **LongMemEval is where a budgeted controller can show value.** LoCoMo mainly tests whether it loses less than a fixed rule does.

The design choices behind the strong systems converge. They store fine-grained units: rounds, extracted facts, or "narrative" facts with timestamps. They search on every query with hybrid retrieval (dense plus BM25, often plus graph or temporal channels), fuse the rankings, rerank, and pack the results up to a token budget ([Hindsight paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12818v1); [Zep paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13956)). EmergenceMem matches the question against single turns, scores each session by its turns, and returns whole sessions ([Emergence blog](https://www.emergence.ai/blog/sota-on-longmemeval-with-rag)). The ablations show where the points come from:

| Component | Measured effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Reading format (JSON + Chain-of-Note) | up to 10 points, even with oracle retrieval | [LongMemEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813) |
| Fact-augmented keys over round values | +9.4% recall@k, +5.4% accuracy | [LongMemEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813) |
| Time-aware query expansion | temporal Recall@10 0.499 → 0.722, but only with a strong LLM | [LongMemEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813) |
| Retrieving k=2 instead of k=1 chunks | about 8–13 judge points | [Mem0 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413) |
| Cross-encoder reranker | −3.4 points when removed | [Emergence blog](https://www.emergence.ai/blog/sota-on-longmemeval-with-rag) |
| Hybrid vs single-channel retrieval, at the top end | 1–2 points | [Agent Zero Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29606) |

Two results matter most for a 7B reader under a budget. Llama-3.1-8B accuracy "drops sharply beyond 3k retrieved tokens" ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)). On LoCoMo, F1 falls when more observations are retrieved: 41.4 at top-5, 37.8 at top-50 ([LoCoMo paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17753)). **More context can hurt a small reader.** Our own numbers agree. FIFO-with-retrieval scores 0.47 on LongMemEval at a 5% budget and 0.42 at 10%. "Keep the last 4 turns, retrieve top 5" scores 0.53 at both budgets ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). So a controller should be allowed to under-fill its budget, and the budget is not the only quantity it should manage.

Results with small open readers are rare, and that is the relevant comparison set for us. With Qwen-2.5-7B on LoCoMo (adversarial excluded), Mem0 scores 53.3 judge accuracy and Memory-R1 61.5 ([Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828)). With Llama-3.1-8B on LongMemEval_S, the best round-plus-fact-key pipeline scores about 0.57, against 0.454 for full context ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)). That 0.57 comes from an appendix table as summarised by a fetch tool, and should be checked against the paper. Our oracle (0.65) and keep-last-4 (0.53) are in that range. Our learned controllers (0.17–0.30) are well below it.

## Learned memory agents learn writes and filtering, from many questions per episode

The learned systems with LoCoMo or LongMemEval numbers share one structural choice: **retrieval is fixed and always on, and learning acts somewhere else**. Memory-R1 always retrieves 60 candidates by similarity. An RL-trained manager chooses ADD/UPDATE/DELETE/NOOP, and a separate RL-trained answer agent "distills" the 60 down to what it uses. Both are trained with PPO or GRPO on only 152 LoCoMo questions with an exact-match reward. With Qwen-2.5-7B, GRPO reaches 61.51 judge and 43.14 F1 ([Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828)). The ablations split the credit. Removing manager RL costs 6.5 F1, and removing answer-agent RL costs 8.5. So part of the gain is in *reading*, not in memory management. Our notes found inconsistent Llama-3.1-8B figures across the paper's versions (judge 62.74 in one table, 59.83 in another), so cite the Qwen numbers. Mem-α learns only writes, and computes its reward through a fixed RAG pipeline over the memory it built ([Mem-α](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1)). PPRO keeps retrieval on and learns only to rewrite the query ([PPRO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00017)).

**MemSkill is the closest precedent for our design, and the best comparison point for the thesis.** It is a lightweight MLP controller, trained with PPO, that picks an *ordered top-K* of memory "skills" through Gumbel-Top-K with the without-replacement joint probability. A frozen 70–80B LLM executes the chosen skills. It is trained on 6 real LoCoMo conversations with about 200 questions each, and it transfers to LongMemEval and to a different executor. It scores 53.82 on LoCoMo and 60.89 on LongMemEval, against 34.58 and 46.81 for Mem0 under the same executor ([MemSkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02474)). Three things separate it from us: it trains on real dialogue, it gets dense multi-question rewards, and its action is "choose K", which cannot output an empty set. On the eviction side, KV Policy is the closest analogue. It uses small per-head MLPs, a hindsight future-utility target, Plackett-Luce ranking trained with REINFORCE leave-one-out, and a reward averaged over all budgets. It beats recency and heavy-hitter heuristics, but it specialises to its training domain. Query-aware heuristics such as SnapKV "achieve similar per-budget costs" when they see the query ([KVP](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10238)). Its supervised ranking losses did *worse* than the RL ranking objective under tight budgets, which is a warning for pure imitation.

These papers handle sparse reward with a few recurring tricks, and **every one of them targets the failure our LongMemEval training hit**. They probe each memory state with many questions: Mem-α uses 4–100 per instance, MemSkill about 200 per LoCoMo conversation, and Memory-R1 one QA reward per pre-question memory state ([Mem-α](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1); [MemSkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02474); [Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828)). MemAgent drops training questions that can be answered without context, which removes about half its data, so the reward always depends on memory ([MemAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02259v1)). AgeMem rewards "information preservation" and penalises overflow ([AgeMem](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01885)). Mem-α rebalanced its data from 4,139 to 562 instances "due to class imbalance". Our LongMemEval episodes carry one question each, so retrieve labels are almost all 0 and the head learns to say no. The teacher also accepts deleting turns nobody asks about, and two of three seeds then fail mostly by deleting evidence ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)).

Evidence that synthetic training transfers to real dialogue is weak or negative. MemAgent and MEM1 were trained on synthetic or composed QA. On Mem-α's MemoryAgentBench they score 0.198 and 0.071, against 0.502 for BM25 top-2 ([Mem-α](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1)). Neural Paging calls cross-domain transfer "an open problem" ([Neural Paging](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02228v1)). Our synthetic-trained policy is the third instance: its retrieve head learned when to fire on templated facts, and conversational turns score differently. Its Mem-α comparison has a useful side lesson. A well-funded learned system (Qwen3-4B, 32 H100s for 3 days) beats BM25 top-2 by only about 0.09 on average. So **"learned ≈ strong retrieval heuristic" on real text is a common result in the field**, not a sign the thesis has failed.

## Retrieval gains come from units, keys and dates, not from gating

The adaptive-retrieval literature predicts our failure. The largest comparison covers 35 methods on 6 QA datasets. In it, learned and uncertainty gates *match* always-retrieve on accuracy and mostly save calls. On multi-hop data the best gates fire on 98–99% of questions anyway. Never-retrieve wins only on TriviaQA, where the answer already sits in the model's weights ([Moskvoretskii et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12835)). In conversational memory the answer is never in the weights, and BM25 over a small archive costs almost nothing. **A gate has nothing to save and everything to lose.** Under a budget, the real decision is *which* items fill a few slots. Causal Memory Policy adds a formal reason: utility cannot be identified for memories that are never retrieved. That happens for 54% of required memories on LongMemEval and 67% on LoCoMo, and the paper's fix is to reserve slots for items sampled with known propensities ([CMP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02070)). A policy that has stopped retrieving cannot even collect the data that would teach it otherwise.

The standard fix for a rare positive class among independent Bernoulli outputs is to change the action. Sampling exactly k items via Gumbel-top-k gives a Plackett-Luce sample without replacement with a tractable log-probability ([Kool et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.06059)). A low-variance unordered-set REINFORCE estimator exists for that sample ([Kool et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06043)). Listwise softmax losses are normalised across candidates, so the positive-to-negative ratio does not matter to them. Focal loss and setting the initial bias to the prior are the fallback if the Bernoulli head stays ([Lin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02002)). REPLUG gives a dense label without RL: train the selector with KL toward the reader's per-candidate likelihood of the gold answer. That gave +5–6% downstream in its paper ([REPLUG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12652)). Nobody has published these estimators on a memory controller's retrieve decision. Applying them here is our inference.

Candidate generation matters on LoCoMo even though it barely matters on LongMemEval. On LongMemEval, BM25 reaches 0.634 session Recall@5 against 0.720 for Stella ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)), and lexical-dense fusion adds nothing significant over BM25 on LongMemEval-S ([Fusion paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04194)). On LoCoMo, our BM25 top-5 finds only **0.35 of needed items, at precision 0.06–0.09** ([EXPERIMENTS.md §6](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). So even perfect gating would cap there. Several levers are known. The fusion paper reports that adding BM25 to a dense late-interaction score gives +8.8 to +17.2 Hit@1 on LoCoMo over dense alone, and +11.2 over BM25 with e5-large-v2, all on CPU. A cross-encoder reranker *hurt* by 6.9 points ([Fusion paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04194)). Our own bge-small run replaced BM25 instead of fusing with it, and recall fell to 0.215. That run also left speaker names out of the embedded text ([EXPERIMENTS.md §6c](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). So the evidence points to fusing, not swapping. Retrieval units matter more than retrievers. On LoCoMo, retrieving LLM-extracted "observations" gives 41.4 F1 at top-5 against 31.7 for raw turns, and temporal F1 is 41.9 against 21.3–26.2 ([LoCoMo paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17753)). Storing facts as the *values* loses information, especially assistant-side content. LightMem scores 32.14 on single-session-assistant questions where NaiveRAG scores 98.21 ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18866)). The safe pattern is LongMemEval's: **facts as extra keys, raw rounds as values**. Time-aware expansion helps temporal questions, but an 8B model "often hallucinat[es]" the time range ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)), so a rule-based date parser is the laptop version.

## Five changes, ranked by expected gain per unit of effort

The diagnosis is structural. A sparse learned gate on a task where gating has no upside, trained on episodes where the gate's label is almost always 0, by a teacher for whom deletion is free. The ranking below follows from that. The gain figures are our estimates, anchored on the heuristics' measured scores. They are not published results. "Laptop" means CPU or the 4 GB GPU. "A40" means rented vLLM evaluation; Experiment 11's whole sweep cost about $2.97 ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)).

| Rank | Change | Where | Effort | Expected gain | You know it worked when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Retrieval floor**: always bring BM25 top-5 (+ follow-the-clue) into a reserved slice at each question; learned policy does eviction and may only *drop* retrieved items | laptop to build; A40 eval (~$1–3) | hours | to heuristic level: LoCoMo ≈0.38–0.40 (+0.05–0.10), LongMemEval ≈0.47–0.53 (+0.2) | retrieval recall equals the heuristics' 0.35 / 0.65 |
| 2 | **Teacher fix**: on benchmarks map delete→archive, or price deleting an item whose future need is unknown; let the policy under-fill the budget | laptop | hours | removes the `evicted` failures of LongMemEval-trained seeds; prevents context bloat past ~3k tokens | `evicted` failures near zero; tokens used ≤ budget |
| 3 | **Pick-k-of-N retrieval head**: Plackett-Luce over a widened shortlist (8→16–20), initialised at BM25 rank order, trained with listwise cross-entropy against gold evidence IDs (LoCoMo evidence turns, LongMemEval `has_answer` turns) | laptop, no LLM | 1–2 days | beats BM25 ordering by a few points; per-question labels are unaffected by one-question episodes | recall@5 above BM25 on held-out conversations before any LLM eval |
| 4 | **Better units on LoCoMo**: speaker name + session date in each indexed turn; BM25 + bge-small score fusion; rule-based date filter; later, 7B-extracted fact keys | rules and fusion on laptop; fact keys one-time on A40 | 1 day (+ ~$1–3) | lifts LoCoMo's 0.35 recall ceiling; observations gave +10 F1 in LoCoMo's own RAG table | BM25-floor recall on LoCoMo rises above 0.35 |
| 5 | **Real multi-question training episodes**: train on a LoCoMo conversation split (~200 Qs each) and pool several LongMemEval questions per episode; drop questions answerable without memory | laptop | 1 day | dense labels for the eviction policy; first fair test of whether learning beats FIFO on real text | learned eviction beats FIFO at matched budget with retrieval floor held fixed |
| 6 | Reader formatting: chronological JSON with dates, Chain-of-Note, retrieved items placed just before the question | A40 eval only | hours | up to 10 points in LongMemEval's ablation; affects all controllers equally | all rows rise together; the ranking does not change |
| 7 | REPLUG-style labels: score each shortlist item by log p(gold \| item, question) under Qwen2.5-7B, then distill into the head | A40 (~16 prompt-logprob passes per question) | 1 day + ~$1–3 | sharper labels than evidence IDs where the annotation is noisy | head agrees with reader preference on held-out questions |
| 8 | GRPO with the 7B reader in the loop on the LoCoMo train split, per-question advantages | A40, many hours | days + $10s | unknown; Memory-R1 needed 4×H100 for LLM-scale policies | only after 1–5 show the learned eviction is worth refining |

The first change should come first because it turns a design argument into a measurement. With the retrieval floor fixed, any difference between the learned controller and FIFO is the eviction policy's own contribution. That is the thesis's actual claim, and right now it is hidden behind the gate failure. It also matches what every learned system in the literature does. If the learned eviction then still loses to FIFO, the problem is the synthetic-to-real shift in the eviction features, and change 5 is the test. Change 3 keeps a learned retrieval decision in the thesis, which preserves RQ-level novelty, but in a form that cannot collapse to "retrieve nothing": the worst it can do is choose a worse five than BM25. KVP and MemSkill are precedents for the action and the estimator ([KVP](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10238); [MemSkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02474)).

Two lower-ranked ideas deserve a sentence each. Compaction is disabled for learned controllers, but "Retain or Consolidate?" reports that consolidation wins by up to 48% absolute under tight budgets and loses under loose ones, with LoCoMo crossing over earlier ([arXiv 2607.17545](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17545)). Re-enabling it at the 2–5% budgets, with summaries kept as *extra* keys rather than replacements, is a natural follow-up once 1–5 are done. Reserving one retrieval slot for a propensity-sampled archive item, as Causal Memory Policy does, gives unbiased data on never-retrieved items at the cost of one slot ([CMP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02070)). It is worth adding when training change 3 with RL rather than imitation.

## Evaluation fixes the thesis needs before any number is credible

Several of our current choices differ from field convention in ways an examiner will catch. **LoCoMo's adversarial category must come out of the headline.** We count it, scoring a refusal as correct ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). Mem0, Zep and Memory-R1 all exclude it because it lacks usable ground truth ([Mem0 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413); [Zep blog](https://www.getzep.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/); [Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828)). Report categories 1–4 as the headline and category 5 separately. Report per category throughout, because controllers differ most on temporal and multi-hop. Use the corrected answer key as a robustness check. An audit found 99 of 1,540 non-adversarial answers wrong, which puts the ceiling near 93.6% ([Penfield audit](https://dev.to/penfieldlabs/we-audited-locomo-64-of-the-answer-key-is-wrong-and-the-judge-accepts-up-to-63-of-intentionally-33lg); [corrected key](https://github.com/dial481/locomo-audit)). State which LoCoMo release is used. The paper describes 50 conversations averaging 9.2k tokens, while the common release has 10 conversations of about 26k ([LoCoMo paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17753); [Mem0 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413)).

**The judge needs an outside check.** Qwen2.5-7B currently grades its own answers. LLM judges prefer low-perplexity text, which includes their own ([Self-preference](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21819v1)). A GPT-4o-mini judge with Mem0's "be generous" prompt accepted 62.81% of deliberately wrong but on-topic answers ([Penfield audit](https://dev.to/penfieldlabs/we-audited-locomo-64-of-the-answer-key-is-wrong-and-the-judge-accepts-up-to-63-of-intentionally-33lg)). Our full-context LoCoMo row already shows the symptom: judge 0.510 but F1 0.312, because long answers pass the judge ([EXPERIMENTS.md §11](../EXPERIMENTS.md)). The cheap fixes follow from that. Re-judge a stratified sample of a few hundred answers with a different-family judge: GPT-4o is LongMemEval's official judge, with >97% human agreement ([LongMemEval repo](https://github.com/xiaowu0162/LongMemEval)). Report the agreement. Run a false-accept test with planted wrong answers. Keep F1 beside the judge score. One reassurance is on record: across three judge rubrics, absolute scores moved but system rankings held ([Anatomy of Agentic Memory](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19320)). So controller rankings are probably safer than absolute numbers. That holds only if answer length does not differ by controller.

**Ceilings and baselines need to be complete and labelled.** LongMemEval has neither a full-context row nor an evidence-only row. The evidence-only reader is cheap (`longmemeval_oracle`) and separates reader limits from controller limits. Published Llama-3.1-8B gets 0.710 on oracle sessions and 0.454 with full context ([LongMemEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10813)). We already served Qwen with YaRN for a 64k LoCoMo run. Extending that to ~115k is our inference about feasibility on an A40, not a tested setting. Otherwise, cite the published full-context figures as references. Report BM25 top-k at a matched token budget, flagged as *query-aware*. KVP showed that query-aware heuristics close most of the gap to learned query-blind policies ([KVP](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10238)). Report the "saturation gap" to full context ([Anatomy of Agentic Memory](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19320)) and the tokens each controller actually uses, since more context hurts 7B readers.

**Splits and uncertainty must hold up to scrutiny.** With 100 held-out LongMemEval questions, a 95% binomial interval at p≈0.5 is about ±0.10. That is enough to separate 0.24 from 0.53, but not to resolve the few-point differences changes 3–7 will produce. Use the cleaned `_s_cleaned` file, report the 30 abstention questions separately ([LongMemEval repo](https://github.com/xiaowu0162/LongMemEval)), and move to cross-validation over all 500 questions in folds. Any LoCoMo-trained controller needs a disclosed conversation-level split, such as MemSkill's 6/2/2 or Memory-R1's 1:1:8 over questions ([MemSkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02474); [Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828)). Its intervals should come from bootstrapping over conversations, because 1,986 questions in 10 conversations are not independent. Mem0 reports mean ± SD over 10 runs ([Mem0 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413)). Our three training seeds should get the same treatment. Finally, the thesis should place its numbers among the small-reader results only, such as Memory-R1's Qwen-7B table and LongMemEval's Llama-8B rows. It should say plainly that the GPT-5-era 90+ figures use different readers and judges and are mostly self-reported.

## Conclusion

The benchmark results change what the thesis should claim. The field's strongest learned systems, from Memory-R1 to MemSkill, never made "whether to retrieve" a learned decision. Our experiment shows why. On real conversations the answer is always in the archive, never in the model, so a gate trained elsewhere learns caution that costs everything and saves nothing. That finding is reportable in its own right. A learned retrieve gate is the wrong abstraction for conversational memory under a budget, and the right one is "always retrieve, learn which k and what to evict". The thesis gains a clean experiment from it: hold retrieval fixed, and measure what the learned eviction policy adds over FIFO.

The second shift is about where the headroom lies. On LoCoMo the oracle only ties full context, and the BM25 shortlist itself finds just a third of the evidence. So LoCoMo mainly tests retrieval units and reading, and LongMemEval is where the controller's budget decisions can show. There the 0.12 gap between keep-last-4 (0.53) and the oracle (0.65) is the prize. The finding that a 7B reader does *worse* with more context suggests the winning controller might use less than its budget. That idea is untested, cheap to test, and absent from the published work.
